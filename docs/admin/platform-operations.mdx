# System, Backup, and Operations

System settings determine how the instance sends email, stores objects, records logs, presents legal information, and performs backups; the operations pages help administrators observe users, models, files, resources, and feedback. Treat them as ongoing operational work, not something to fill in once at first install.

## SMTP and email

Fill in the SMTP host, port, username, password, sender address, and TLS policy under "System → Email service". Email is usually used for account verification, password resets, and operational notifications, so configure it early in a full deployment that serves real users.

When configuring:

- Use the SMTP credentials provided by your service provider or an app-specific password; don't reuse the administrator password.
- The sender address should belong to a domain you control, with the necessary SPF, DKIM, DMARC, or provider verification configured.
- Distinguish the ports required for direct TLS vs. STARTTLS and fill them in per the provider's requirements.
- After saving, verify sending, receiving, verification-code validity, and the password reset flow with a non-administrator test mailbox.

When SMTP is not configured, features that depend on email may not complete. Don't troubleshoot by printing verification codes or SMTP passwords into public logs.

## Storage, uploads, and retention

"Storage and uploads" configures application objects, upload limits, and object storage integration. A single node can use local storage; when you need multiple replicas, cross-node access, or external parsing, use a controlled object storage service.

Before defining upload and retention policies, decide: the maximum single-file size, allowed types, total space per user, how long previews and artifacts are kept, whether there is a recovery window after deletion, and whether backups cover the objects. Raising limits must be paired with checking reverse-proxy request body limits, in-app limits, parser service capacity, disk, and backup costs.

Object storage credentials and bucket configuration are sensitive. Use a separate least-privilege account rather than granting administrator rights over the entire cloud account; after changing endpoints, buckets, regions, or keys, test upload, download, preview, parsing, sharing, and deletion.

## Legal, contact information, logs, and privacy

Maintain the terms of service, privacy policy, contact entry points, and log/privacy policies in the system settings. A public service should provide real, actionable contact information and a privacy statement consistent with its actual data flows.

The goal of a log policy is to let administrators diagnose problems, not to store more sensitive information. Principles include:

- don't log model provider API keys, OAuth Client Secrets, payment signatures, JWTs, refresh tokens, complete user files, or complete private prompts.
- when troubleshooting, prefer timestamps, request IDs, sanitized error codes, service names, and aggregated metrics.
- set reasonable retention and access permissions; log exports may also contain user metadata.
- establish owners and approval workflows for data exports, deletions, backup restores, and transfers to third-party services.

## Announcements and content operations

Announcements suit maintenance notices, feature tips, and service status updates. Before publishing, use a test account to check the display scope, dismissal behavior, mobile layout, and links; urgent announcements should be concise, avoiding embedded sensitive internal information or unverifiable external scripts.

Prompts, skills, image styles, and speech configurations directly affect user experience and cost. Validate any change intended for all users on a small set of accounts first, roll it out gradually, and keep a path to quickly disable or roll it back.

## Backup, migration, and configuration export

"Backup and migration" can generate data migration archives and export administrator configuration. The two differ in risk and purpose:

| Type | Typically contains | Primary purpose | Sensitivity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full data backup | Business data, vectors, and selected files/artifacts | Disaster recovery, migration, restore testing | Highly sensitive |
| Administrator configuration export | Settings, channels, models, identity providers, user groups, payment/storage/email configuration | Replicating controlled environments, change archiving | Highly sensitive, often contains credentials |

A backup isn't finished once it's created successfully. At minimum, regularly verify a restore in an isolated environment and check administrator sign-in, normal conversations, attachments, knowledge bases, vectors, OAuth, email, and any needed tools. Before restoring, stop writes, keep a copy of the original data, and record the version and scope; don't use `docker compose down -v` as a routine restart command—it deletes named volumes.

The Personal edition should back up `DATA_DIR` as a whole. The Full edition must also protect the database, Redis, Qdrant, sandbox archive volumes, and `DATA_DIR`. For detailed commands and the restore order, see [Upgrades, Backups, and Recovery](../deployment/upgrade-backup.mdx).

## Usage, analytics, resources, and feedback

"Analytics" helps you observe active users, requests, and cost trends; "Usage records" shows model calls and metering; "Content resources", "Files", and "Feedback" help you manage documents, images, workspace resources, uploaded objects, and user issues.

Recommended periodic operational rhythm:

1. Check error rates, model failures, payment anomalies, and high-cost users/models daily or weekly.
2. Review growth trends, storage usage, knowledge base task backlogs, and abnormally large files.
3. Tie user feedback to actual conversations, models, channels, or feature configurations, but avoid copying private content into public tickets.
4. Confirm backups and retention rules before cleaning up test files, abandoned templates, and expired redemption codes.
5. For long tasks such as vector rebuilds, bulk deletions, and imports/exports, watch progress and results before starting the next round.

The operations pages provide observation and management capabilities, not host-level monitoring. Production instances should still monitor disk, memory, container restarts, outbound network, backup success, and certificate expiry; when a trend looks off, first restrict the risky capability or user scope, then investigate root causes.
